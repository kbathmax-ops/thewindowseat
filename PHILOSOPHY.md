# The Window Seat — Philosophy

Three travelers stand behind this product. None of them traveled to relax. Each
went out to get something specific and bring it back.

---

## 1. The three figures

### Anthony Bourdain — empathy, taken at the table

Bourdain's argument was that moving toward other people is itself the point, and
that food is the fastest way in. Stated plainly on *Parts Unknown*:

> "If I'm an advocate for anything, it's to move. As far as you can, as much as
> you can. Across the ocean, or simply across the river. The extent to which you
> can walk in someone else's shoes or at least eat their food is a plus for
> everybody."

He was equally clear that this costs you something, and that the cost is the
mechanism, not a side effect. From *No Reservations: Around the World on an Empty
Stomach* (Bloomsbury, 2007):

> "Travel isn't always pretty. It isn't always comfortable. Sometimes it hurts,
> it even breaks your heart. But that's okay. The journey changes you; it should
> change you."

**Principle: discomfort is the tuition.** A trip that changed nothing about you
was a purchase, not a journey.

### Marco Polo — curiosity, recorded honestly

Polo's *Travels* opens by addressing anyone "who desire to get knowledge of the
various races of mankind and of the diversities of the sundry regions of the
World." What follows in that same prologue is, remarkably, a sourcing standard
(Yule translation, Prologue, ch. 1):

> "Some things indeed there be therein which he beheld not; but these he heard
> from men of credit and veracity. And we shall set down things seen as seen, and
> things heard as heard only, so that no jot of falsehood may mar the truth of
> our Book."

He catalogues coal-heated bathhouses, paper money, and unfamiliar rites without
recoiling from them. Difference is inventory, not threat.

**Principle: describe faithfully, judge little, and label your sources.**

### Xuanzang — truth, at the price it costs

Xuanzang left Tang China in 629 CE **in defiance of an imperial ban on foreign
travel**, because the Chinese translations of Buddhist scripture available to him
were riddled with what he saw as contradictions and discrepancies. He would not
settle the question secondhand. He went to the source, spent sixteen years away,
and returned in 645 CE with at least 657 Sanskrit texts carried on twenty
packhorses. The *Great Tang Records on the Western Regions* was written afterward,
at the emperor's urging, as a record of what he had actually seen.

**Principle: go to the source; a secondhand account is not knowledge.**

---

## 2. The unifying thesis

**All three traveled to acquire something and carry it home.** Bourdain went for
empathy, Polo for knowledge, Xuanzang for truth. Not one of them framed the
journey as escape, as reward, or as a list of sights survived. Travel was a
method — the most expensive and most reliable method — of learning something you
could not learn where you already stood.

> The Window Seat exists to name the lesson before the destination.

A place is not a prize. It is a teacher with a particular curriculum, and some
curricula suit some people at some moments better than others. That is the whole
premise of the match.

---

## 3. Three product principles

**1. Name the lesson, not the landmark.**
Every recommendation leads with what a place tends to teach. The Treasury at
Petra is not the recommendation; *hospitality is the oldest rule of the desert*
is the recommendation, and Petra is where you go to learn it. Landmarks appear
only as the setting for a lesson, never as the payoff.

**2. Things seen as seen — no fabrication.**
Polo's rule is our rule. Every lesson traces to a real, linked, first-person
essay. Verbatim quotes are marked verbatim; paraphrases are marked as
paraphrases. If we cannot find real writing about a place, that place is not in
the database. No model writes copy at runtime — the match is deterministic, and
the source is always one click away.

**3. Difference is an invitation, not a warning.**
Following Polo's register and Bourdain's manners: the unfamiliar is described as
worth going toward. Discomfort is named honestly — Bourdain would not forgive us
for pretending otherwise — but it is framed as the cost of the lesson, never as a
hazard to be managed. The user is a guest, not an inspector.

---

## 4. What this rules out

- Superlatives and rankings. No "top 10", no "hidden gems", no "bucket list".
- Copy that sells a feeling instead of naming a lesson.
- Any lesson we cannot point to a source for.
- Framing a country as an experience consumed rather than a teacher engaged.
